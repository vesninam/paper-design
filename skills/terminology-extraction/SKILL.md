# Terminology extraction

Build the conceptual context needed to ask the research question correctly, rather than a dictionary of every technical word.

## Inputs and source handling

Read the research question and any provided papers or citations. Work in the paper-specific workspace; ask about an ambiguous destination before writing. Do not edit framework or skill files.

Prefer approximately three representative papers spanning the concepts relevant to the question; use fewer or more when justified. If papers are not supplied, retrieve suitable primary literature using available search tools. Verify titles, authors, dates, and identifiers from the actual sources. Record whether access is to full text, an abstract, or another limited extract. If retrieval is unavailable, work only from available material and state the coverage limit rather than inventing sources.

## Extraction

1. Select concepts whose interpretation affects the question, task boundaries, methods, or conclusions.
2. Extract definitions with source locations where available. Paraphrase faithfully and preserve qualifications, units, notation, and conditions of applicability.
3. Identify related concepts that are easily conflated, such as sampling interval versus effective resolution. Explain the practical consequence of the distinction for this investigation.
4. Compare usage across papers. Preserve disagreements and discipline-specific meanings instead of asserting a false consensus.
5. Separate source-supported definitions from your synthesis and from proposed project-specific operational definitions. Flag any consequential definition that needs the researcher's decision.

## Artifact and completion

Write or update `terminology.md` with a source inventory and entries containing:

| Term | Definition and scope | Related concepts | Important distinction | Relevance to our question | Sources |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Add unresolved ambiguities and suggested wording changes to the question or tasks. Do not silently reframe those artifacts. Completion means the main conceptual distinctions needed for the investigation are explicit and traceable, with access limitations and unsettled meanings visible.
