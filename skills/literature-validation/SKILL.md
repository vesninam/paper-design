# Literature validation

Position the proposed investigation against existing research. Produce a critical comparison, not a stack of paper summaries or a guarantee of novelty.

## Context and retrieval

Read the question, tasks, result specifications, evidence matrix, and paper map when available. A concrete standalone plan is also sufficient; state its missing context. Work in the identified paper workspace only, leaving framework and skills unchanged.

Use available literature search or browsing tools to retrieve and verify relevant primary research. If retrieval is unavailable, analyze supplied sources and mark the validation incomplete; do not imply that a fresh search occurred. Record the search date, search services, queries, selection rationale, and access limits. Full-text access and abstract-only access support different levels of assessment.

## Investigation

1. Translate task aspects, proposed contributions, and critical methodological assumptions into targeted search questions. Search synonyms and neighboring terminology to avoid missing equivalent work under different names.
2. Identify foundational work, the closest prior investigations, and relevant recent research. Use reviews to discover primary studies, and citation trails to check important omissions. Bound the search by scientific relevance rather than an arbitrary paper count.
3. Verify bibliographic details and inspect the source passage supporting each consequential comparison. Distinguish what a paper actually studied from your inference about its relevance. Never invent references, findings, identifiers, or full-text access.
4. Compare systems, conditions, variables, methods, metrics, validation, and limitations with the proposed result specifications. Treat an apparently conflicting result as a question about methods or scope before declaring a contradiction.
5. Classify each proposed contribution as established, an extension, a potentially unresolved gap, or uncertain, with sources and rationale. Not finding a study is a bounded search finding, not proof that no study exists.
6. Identify reusable methods, missing controls or baselines, unnecessary duplication, and claims requiring narrower scope. Explain how each recommended revision affects specific task IDs, result IDs, evidence rows, or map nodes.

## Artifact and completion

Write `state-of-the-art.md` with:

- Search scope, date, queries, source inventory, and access limitations.
- Established knowledge and unresolved or contested questions.
- A comparison matrix: aspect or proposed claim, prior findings and citations, our task/result IDs, difference or overlap, and implication.
- The intended contribution, methodological implications, and remaining uncertainty.
- Prioritized proposed changes with affected artifacts and decision status.

Use traceable citations with verified DOI or source URLs and locators for consequential claims where available. Keep source-supported statements separate from synthesis and recommendations.

Apply revisions to other paper artifacts when requested or already authorized, retaining stable IDs and decision history. Otherwise record them as proposals; do not silently alter the question to manufacture novelty. Completion means the contribution and methodology can be explained relative to the retrieved research and necessary revisions are explicit. Report unresolved search limits rather than declaring the design validated beyond the available evidence.
